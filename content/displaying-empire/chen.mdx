# Consuming Exotic Leisure: The Snake Charmer through the Frame of the Picturesque

*Muyang Chen*

In 1812, British writer James Forbes published the first edition of his
travelogue *Oriental Memoirs: A Narrative of Seventeen Years Residence
in India*, offering an account of his nearly two decades in India as a
British East India Company official.[^1] His son-in-law, Marc René de
Montalembert, a French émigré who had served in the British Army, was
credited as the draftsman of several printed illustrations. By focusing
on one of those prints that purported to be based on drawings made on
site, *Dancing Snake and Musicians* (1812; fig. 1) is emblematic of the
depiction of exoticized leisure that makes palpable the British
consumption of Indian customs. This fabrication is constructed through a
complex interplay between visual genres that grapple with the
translation of the unfamiliar by adjusting familiar aesthetic
parameters.[^2]

The print depicts an exoticized scene in an Indian bazaar. In the left
half of the print, a crowd gathers around a snake charmer sitting on the
ground and playing his *bīn*, a traditional Indian wind instrument.
Driven by the tune, a serpent (nāga) next to the snake charmer rises
from the ground, forming an elegantly curved form. Short engraved lines
and dots render the fine scales of the nāga, as well as the
spectacle-shaped pattern on its hood that indicates its lethal
poisonousness, juxtaposed with its tamed gesture. Another musician is
playing an Amtr̥i, a string instrument that fell out of use in the late
eighteenth century, to accompany the performance.[^3] While the scene is
set against a blank background, the liveliness of the bazaar is
underscored by the figures behind the crowd. In contrast to the sharp
delineation of figures in the foreground, these figures are rendered in
less detail and with softer, granular lines. The reduced tonal variation
and contrast make the background figures appear blurry, suggesting a
greater distance from the artist. A vegetable seller and a man wearing
turban are sitting underneath a parasol. The seller casually holds a
water pipe, while the other figure sits sideways, turning his head
toward the viewer. Their sight lines indicate that they recognize the
artist's gaze while simultaneously creating a sense of interaction for
the viewer.

The snake charmer is a primary subject in the exoticized depiction of
the sensorial "Orient" and was central to European interest.[^4] The
snake charmer in the print functions as a quintessential signpost like
the banyan tree and the elephant, crystallizing the complexity of
colonial life into a "pleasing and graspable vision" for the viewer to
enjoy.[^5] The lethal nāga, a visual symbol of unruly wilderness for the
European eye, is presented as tamed by the snake charmer's musical
power, emphasizing its role as a docile performer in sensory spectacle
and leisure.[^6] Furthermore, the snake charmer, with the mythical
ability to control the serpent, is often compared to the serpent itself
for both possessing the power to manipulate animals, thus foregrounding
the civilized Europeans in contrast with the primitive "Orient".[^7] The
subduing of exoticized unruliness to provide visual pleasure creates a
doubling between the subject matter of the print and the viewing
experience for the audience: as the crowd enjoys the performance of the
nāga, the viewer is positioned as an audience to be pleased by the
spectacle of exotic leisure.

The depiction of the snake charmer as a specimen reflects the burgeoning
visual accounts of ethnographic subjects. Often compiled as albums that
attempt to organize Indian society into a simplified visual hierarchy
for consumption, these accounts sit at the intersection of artistic
production and ethnographic inquiry.[^8] An example can be found in an
opaque watercolor of the snake charmer and his wife (fig. 2), published
around the 1830s as part of an album of ninety-two drawings categorizing
Indian castes and occupations.[^9] In the painting, the snake charmer is
dressed in a lavish red and golden sash and flamboyant feathers tucked
in his turban. Both he and his wife are rendered with elaborate golden
ornaments. The depiction reflects an aesthetics of precision that aims
to form the typical through detailed observation.[^10] The same
aesthetics are evident in the print, where each figure is characterized
by a different social type: the soldier with a long sword hanging at his
waist, the guard holding a long staff, or the vendor walking alongside
his ox. However, the print departs from the depiction of the snake
charmer as the flamboyant entertainer, subduing such typological
characteristics through his plain clothing while retaining the central
elements that mark his occupation. The monochromatic palette of the
print and the minimalist depiction, in fact, enhance the documentary
quality of a sketch when compared to the elaborate watercolor. Further,
while the watercolor drawing removes the figures from any specific
context to fixate on the unfamiliarity of the individuals, the print, in
comparison, creates an interactive scene by compiling "specimens" of
individuals from different tiers of society in a bazaar, creating a
romanticized vision of the everyday.[^11]

The sense of authenticity is further underscored by the inscription
beneath the image: "From a drawing taken on the spot by Baron de
Montalembert in 1807." The draftsperson referred to is Marc René de
Montalembert, who served as the aide-de-camp to General Sir John
Cradock, the Commander-in-Chief of the Madras Army.[^12] Montalembert's
engagement in military maneuvers is key to his access and mobility. This
limited access, mostly available to military and company officers,
enabled these artists to claim of authority over the subjects they
depicted. The use of soft-ground etching, a technique that was popular
from the eighteenth century to the 1820s, further contributes to the
construction of eyewitness account.[^13] By adding a layer of tallow,
the ground is kept tacky, preventing it from being lifted as a whole
piece and creating fragmented lines with a granular texture that yields
the effect of a pencil drawing.[^14] In this print, the grainy lines are
largely used for shading, especially in creating roughly sketched
impressions of figures in the back, with varying degrees of detail that
underline the sense of immediacy. Besides subject matter, the
printmaking technique is also a key contributor to the viewing
experience by creating subtle yet rich variations in tone and texture.
The print combines etching and engraving, as well as a sophisticated
combination of hatching, cross-hatching, and stippling, to enrich the
image despite its lack of color.

*Dancing Snake and Musicians* demonstrates a strategic reinvention of
the depiction of ethnographic subjects through the frame of the
picturesque, through which India is visualized for the British audience
to understand. The print is purported to be an authentic eyewitness
account through its construction of an everyday scene, the monochrome
palette, and the use of soft-ground etching to yield the effect of
pencil-sketched lines. However, it involves the complex work of cultural
reconfiguration and translation. As art historian Romita Ray puts it,
"the picturesque was driven by the desire to make nature 'do' something,
not just 'be.'"[^15] By anchoring the snake charmer as a palpable
signifier of exotic leisure, the print renders a bazaar scene of the
everyday where encounters with different occupations occur. Along with
the subtle yet complex variation in tone and texture, the print provides
an exoticized view that satisfies curiosity yet renders manageable for
comfortable consumption, fulfilling the imagination of colonial life.
