# *Indian Spices for English Tables*: British Satire in Colonial Media

*Anna Ikle-Maizlish*

Published in 1860, George Francklin Atkinson's *Indian spices for
English tables* is a 27-page leather-bound predecessor to the modern
graphic novel. 120 satirical sketches, arranged across the pages and
reproduced *en masse* using a pen-and-ink lithographic process, feature
humorous scenes from the author's travels across the Indian
subcontinent. The author, a member of the Bengal Engineers in the
British East Indian Company's military, writes a distinctly colonialist
interpretation of his travels, using humor to present local practices
and Indian locals as inferior to him and his "knowing" British
colleagues. Published by the British-based Day & Son and meant for broad
distribution, the book contributes to a legacy of British satirical
caricature and literature. However, unlike other British satire, *Indian
spices* does not focus on critiques of British society as much as it
satirizes Indian society and the struggles British officers face in many
unruly travel scenarios. In this respect, *Indian spices for English
tables* sits within a niche genre of paternalistic British colonial
satire, where caricature is used as a colonial tool to spread soft power
through mockery, orientalism, and degradation of local ways of life as a
justification for colonial presence. Through accessible visuals,
first-hand accounts, and satire, Atkinson's *Indian Spices for English
Tables* frames colonial experience in a consumable spectacle for British
audiences. The book shows how British imperial power depended not only
on political and military domination but on aesthetic practices that
centered British authority in an orientalized, "inferior" India.

*Indian spices for English tables* was published three years after the
Indian-led Sepoy Rebellion of 1857—a turning point in British colonial
rule that brought the downfall of the Mughal dynasty and reshaped
British control. Despite this, the book notably avoids representations
of direct military power and battle. The book's themes are presented
through its illustrated title page [(fig. 1)](#fig1): shaky methods of
transportation break down on the rugged terrain, risky attempts to ride
exotic animals, and the dutiful service of many local Indian servants
who accompany Atkinson throughout his arduous experiences in rural
India. The illustration centers Atkinson's figure against "Our Special
Correspondent," emphasizing first-handedness and building authenticity
through the overt identification of authorial voice. Atkinson's
journal-like first hand observations are central to the resulting
narrative. His British perspective is centered through comments that
emphasize India as an exotic, unserious society. On plate 16 [(fig. 2)](#fig2),
he observes women "leisurely composing themselves to reap," comments
that Indian rural labor traditions seem "not very arduous to attain,"
and calls Indian native carts "a very picturesque sort of vehicle."[^1]
The captions, along with his reference to rural populations as the "rustics," portray Indian society
as lackadaisical, antiquated, and crude and establish colonial authority
through observation. The sketchy, journalistic style of the drawings
support a sense of legitimacy. These value judgements, supported through
visual representations of locals in minimal clothing, give no mention of
the local social, political, or military systems in place—ones that
gave rise to the significant Sepoy Rebellion.

Several images further support the narrative's legitimacy: on the final
page Atkinson laments about the sub-par conditions and tropical troubles
of his travelling arrangements, socializes with other "knowing ones"
(how British officers are called throughout the book), views the
battlefield, and finally returns home to transcribe his adventures for
the European audience [(fig. 3)](#fig3). Atkinson's explicit illustration of his own writing process—one of
many throughout the pages—communicates to the reader that the book is
an accurate and legitimate portrayal of events in India, bolstering the
authenticity of the narrative. Still, Atkinson folds humor into the story—
the first two images on the final page show his contorted body
tripping over tent-poles and a "turgid" face covered in mosquito bites.
Atkinson's serious treatment of British presence and his writing
contrasts with satirical retellings of his experiences in India to
create an interesting dichotomy: one where the author and his foreign
environment are comedically represented while the colonial presence they
represent is unquestioned. Fitting into a long legacy of "civilizing"
missions of imperialism, the book uses satire to emphasize the
disorderliness of Indian society, rationalizing the need for British
colonial intervention.

The book's publisher, London-based Day & Son, is announced on the title
page as "Lithographers to the Queen," emphasizing the book's access and
colonial mission in distribution. This level of access for, essentially,
a comic book underscores the importance of satire to colonial culture.
As outlined by Dahlia Bhattacharya, British caricature culture travelled
to India in the nineteenth century with periodicals like *Punch*
(1841-1992), which adopted colonial subjects particularly in the wake of
the Revolt of 1857.[^2] One such illustration, 1858's *The New Year's
Gift*, represents the British government's success in the aftermath of
the uprising. The caricature presents India as an exotic and menacing
tiger next to well-dressed English politicians [(fig. 4)](#fig4).[^3] Expressing
British governmental unease about the feasibility of maintaining a
colony in India, the image subjugates India as a wild beast,
simultaneously commenting on British domestic politics and redirecting
the satirical subject to the colonized population. Bhattacharya's
argument that colonial satire reinforced racial hierarchies and imperial
authority is supported by Atkinson's persistent mocking of Indian
customs and portrayal of British officers as competent observers. While
later Anglo-Indian works use humor to expose colonial contradictions and
the fragility of imperial authority.[^4] Atkinson's humor redirects
colonial anxieties into individual comedic mishaps, leaving the broader
imperial system uncontested. Referential to British literary traditions,
satire becomes a tool of imperial knowledge and reassurance.

Reference to satirical tradition is no clearer than in the dedication of
*Curry and Rice*, Atkinson's 1859 illustrated vignette-style account of
colonial life also published by Day & Son. *Curry and Rice* differs from
*Indian Spices* in both style and focus, offering more extended written
commentary and depicting a broader range of colonial British experiences
beyond Atkinson's own travels. In a dedication, Atkinson attests that
the book is an homage to the legacy of William Thackeray, famed British
satirist of British elite society. Atkinson explicitly positions himself
in a British literary tradition that critiques society through humor and
exaggeration, only he uses the genre not for self-deprecating critique
but for imperialist vindication. Swati Chattopadhyay interrogates this
propagandist mission through analysis of one of *Curry and Rice*'s
illustrations [(fig. 5)](#fig5), arguing that Atkinson represents Indian
domestic spaces as primitive and unsophisticated as generalizable
characteristics of local society, aiming to stir shock and ridicule
within British audiences.[^5] The messy kitchen, exotic cooking
practices, "ape-like" figures, and emphasis on difference and disorder
in the image serve to associate the contrasting British society with
cleanliness, racial superiority, and technological advantage. Through
humorous depictions of everyday life, Atkinson disarms the reader and
creates associations of a disorderly India and a rational Britain.

*Indian spices for English tables* weaponizes existing British satirical
language to translate colonial observations into humorous spectacle.
Lithographic reproduction, approachable language, and accessible visual
aesthetics transformed propaganda into everyday entertainment for
British audiences, portraying India as a picturesque, inferior,
exoticized land. In the decades following Atkinson's book, satire was reappropriated as a form of colonial
critique. Indian satirical magazines such as *Basantak*, *Hindi Punch,*
and *Oudh Punch*[^6] adopted British caricature forms and, at times,
illustrations to critique colonial authority, a direct result of the
aesthetic interaction established by Atkinson's works. Circulated beyond
British control, the visual language of satire eventually was adapted by
Indian artists and periodicals themselves for purposes of political
commentary. Atkinson's *Indian spices for English tables* thus exemplifies how the
British empire was sustained not only by military authority but by
aesthetic practices that shaped how colonial presence was seen,
circulated, and ultimately contested.
