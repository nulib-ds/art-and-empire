# Shades of Empire: Pigments as Anti-Imperialist Battlegrounds

*Louis Chavey*

In *Seated Woman Sewing*, the fabric of what seems to be a casual,
folk-style *ghagral* pleated skirt is spread over a woman's leg as she
sews. Similarly, in *Seated Man Making Lanterns*, traditional Indian
lanterns are made by fixing what seems to be lightweight fabric or paper
surrounding the man onto thin, yellow wooden or bamboo supports; above
are already finished products. Painted on the silicate mineral mica,
these pocket-sized miniatures were typically sold in a set at a
commercial marketplace as a sort of travel souvenir. With their
non-descript backgrounds, these miniatures followed in the artistic
tradition of *firka* (series of paintings). Demand for these sets was
largely driven by European markets' desire for ethnographic images of
Indian occupations, habits, and daily life. Naturally, these artists
catered their style slightly to European audiences through elements like
perspective, light, and shade[^1]—seen through the shadow
cast to the right of the woman and her ghagral, as well as the shadowing
on the man's lanterns. There was not a complete shift towards
European style, however, as precise brushwork and organizing isolated
figures into visual patterns across the set were retained from Mughal
lineage.[^2] Regardless of whether the Indian yellow used here was
industrially or traditionally produced, its use alongside Indigo to
highlight craft labor subverts these pigments' histories of industrial
exploitation and in sustaining colonial British rule. Distinguishing the
ghagral and lamps from their respective backgrounds, these pigments
further the other formal qualities connecting the miniatures to Mughal
court painting by asserting this Indian artistic identity in spite of
colonial British influence.

Commissioned by the British East India Company during the 18th and 19th
century (termed *Company paint*), a new addition to the Northwestern
Special Collections shows the extraction of resources—both natural
and cultural—that characterized Britain's colonization of India.
Although mediums among the works may vary, they all nevertheless convey
Britain's romanticized fascination with India at the time. By
illuminating the connections between artistic usage and economic
production of the pigments Indian yellow and Indigo, respectively, in
imperialist Company works, a reassertion of local prowess under colonial
rule lies in disrupting the economic systems of pigment production.

The use of Indian yellow, also known as *puree*, shows that when
economic production impinges upon cultural and religious beliefs, the
resulting mobilization against its production deconstructs European
representational conventions. Originally produced in Bihar and Bengal,
*puree* was made from the urine of cows solely fed mango leaves.
Although Indian artists largely rejected the resulting pigment, the
cruelty of production was largely shielded from Europe as "Indian yellow
was...praised for its fineness in conveying the subtleties of skin
colors...especially useful in creating a dark brown flesh tone."[^3]
This dark brown skin tone was used to depict Indians in many Company
works, ranging from small background figures in large-scale sceneries to
even works similar to *Seated Woman Sewing* and *Seated Man Making
Lanterns*. Regardless of how much spatial focus was given to an Indian
subject these miniatures, seldom were they ever afforded much artistic
focus: "The isolation of figures from any landscape or narrative
setting, as well as the relative stasis of their poses, freezes them as
objects for scientific observation."[^4] In part, repeated use of
Indian yellow in dark brown skin tones helped to standardize the real
Indian people into what were essentially data points for European
consumers. As the production of puree heightened to match growing
European demand, Indigenous Cow Protection Societies threatened to
destabilize British rule, as outrage flared into communal conflicts by
the late 19th century.[^5] In response to the civil unrest, "the British
palette had changed, and a particular mode of depicting colonized
subjects \[with Indian yellow\] had diminished aesthetic appeal
precisely because the process of acquiring the raw materials to produce
these depictions had itself become a threat to British rule."[^6] In
this sense, resistance against British suppression of religious
principles also becomes a fight against the aesthetic exoticization of
'Indian types' in Company art because both systems—legislated
religious suppression and aesthetic tastes reducing Indians into types—can be seen as supporting each other: Justification for this pigment
production also highlights how these practices were viewed as
sacrilegious behavior towards a culturally revered animal. Indian
assertion of respecting Hindu religious traditions is also an assertion
of meaningful identification and representation, beyond just being
generalized colonial subjects with no agency.

Producing Indigo, another key pigment, also created a gruesome impact on
Indian bodies that necessitated going beyond mere disruption of
industrial indigo production to artisanal methods recalibrating
laborers' relationships with the pigment. Prized as a brilliant blue
dye, Indigo was produced from *Indigofera tinctoria* leaves steeped in
alkaline (*khari*) and sweet (*mithā*) vats, then fermented or boiled
and agitated to oxidize and collect the indigo-blue sediment.[^7] As
British East India Company presence grew to meet European demand,
however, production was scaled and mechanized into factories.[^8] In
many landscape works commissioned by the British East India Company,
Indigo was often used to paint dark spaces with a "calm and solemn"
shade[^9]. Packaged within a palatable pictorial aesthetic, these works
used detached, quasi-scientific and militaristic observation to
encourage commercial buy-in to the ideal of an uncolonized India.[^10]
These landscapes staged an imperial fantasy of conquest, with indigo
shading the distant horizon beneath clouds to deepen the work's mystery.
That Indigo was a diffused, looming presence in the shadows within
imperialist depictions all too closely mirrors its marks of suffering on
the Indian bodies economically subjugated to produce it: "Labouring in
indigo vats at night, or hidden in Western factories, barracks, gaols,
shipped with bodies to imperial wars, its intense saturation could and
did disguise sweat, dirt, blood and tears."[^11] The reality of this
imperial glory that Indigo helped promise was built from a similar
shrouding of "sap, sweat, blood from the whip" embedded in the final
product.[^12] As planters moved operations north to Bihar after earlier
revolts, the same forced indigo system (*tinkathia*) persisted until the
Gandhi-led *Champaran Satyagraha* of 1917, a non-violent peasant protest
that abolished tinkathia due to planter abuses.[^13] With Gandhi
championing self-sufficient, domestic, and thus strictly
anti-industrialist production of textiles, Indigo production followed
suit: "Champaran's indigo agitation \[was linked\] to contemporary
handloom practice, displaying naturally dyed textiles and small-vat
experiments...thereby reframing indigo as a medium of craft resilience
rather than plantation extraction."[^14] Indigo goes further than
Indian yellow in illustrating that pigment's usage in art is intertwined
with dismantling the same economic systems producing it: A symbol of
economic resistance must also reframe how the goods are used to offer an
alternative vision of the future—of Indigo as a slowly produced,
sacred pigment as opposed to just another economic good supporting an
imperialist vision.

Ultimately, reasserting core aspects of Indian culture—Hindu
protections of cows with Indian yellow and incorporating Indigo
production into self-sustaining, craftsmanlike traditions predating
industrialization—reclaims economic systems of pigment production for
domestic, not colonial, benefit through means that deconstruct the very
imperialist ideals propagated through art that were supporting these
systems. Although pigments allow artists to imagine new realities and
redefine existing ones, their production is still rooted in this reality
and the economic systems supporting it. If new art hopes to better
realize new conceptions of reality, the systems producing the means for
this imagination—the pigments, canvases, and other art materials—should align with the hopes and values fueling artists' imaginations.
