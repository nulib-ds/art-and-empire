# Positioning Exoticism: The Picturesque and its Visual Mediations in Thomas Daniell's *A Hindoo Place of Worship*

*Yuetong (Alysa) Xia*

*A Hindoo Place of Worship* is an aquatint by Thomas Daniell, engraved
in 1804 by Joseph Stadler, and sold in 1805 in Britain by Edward Orme.
During Daniell's seven-year journey in India from 1786 to 1793, the
artist created this landscape painting in the style of the picturesque,
an aesthetic device that emphasizes naturalistic qualities of
landscapes. Focusing on qualities of beauty and harmony, the picturesque
not only enabled artists to create romanticized views of India, but they
also showed how travel illustrations were mediums to conduct broader
scientific inquiry.[^1]

This work conformed to the conventions of landscape painting with its
tripartite composition with a "darkened foreground, highlighted middle
distance, and background."[^2] The prominent midground hosts central
figures engaging in ritual activities around a vertical pillar, one
standing upright, one seated and the other bowing. This Hindu shrine of
worship, indicated by the title, is surrounded by nature in the
foreground and background. Collectively, nature establishes a S-shaped
path for the viewer's gaze to follow, starting from the patches of
darker grass in the lower-right to the clouds of the upper right. The
use of the picturesque reveals to the modern audience the tensions in
the artist's role in the growing British mercantile control of the
Indian subcontinent. Daniell presents a British topographical account of
the Indian landscape that integrates its exoticism into the familiar
British visual language and negotiates visual accuracy with a
perspective that legitimized further British control.

The picturesque presents a vision of harmony and beauty with
"fundamental desire to stabilize disorienting currency of the alien
through familiar cultural paradigms and practices."[^3] Capturing an
unfamiliar Indian landscape and transforming it into a "pure
landscape,"[^4] Daniell reorganizes its components to suit the British
audience. He simplified his portrayal of each of the unfamiliar
dark-skinned human figures, who wore unique headdresses and clothing
that only covered their lower bodies. Grouping them around the central
pillar, Daniell further constructed a zoomed-out perspective to capture
the wider Indian landscape, displayed in the grass, trees and clouds in
this composition. This perspective allowed human figures to appear
compositionally less significant than the landscape, thus subsuming the
unfamiliarity of the subject matter with the picturesque composition and
maintaining the landscape's decorum by subduing such exoticism in the
picturesque aesthetic.

These visual rearrangements of the Indian landscape in the picturesque
tradition displayed a negotiation between narrative accuracy and the
Company's mercantile desires. Details in the shrubbery and trees
indicated the usage of the camera obscura on-site in the design process
where Daniell traced outlines of the projected inverted image in the
dark box.[^5] Claiming more accuracy via rendering these botanical
differences, such practice emphasized how the eyewitness account "endows
the imagery with historical specificity -- it was made 'in this place',
'at that time' -- and yet dissociates the artist/author from any single
location or perspective ... a sort of imperial flâneur."[^6] Daniell's
depiction of human figures without making eye contact with each other
allows this scene to appear incidental, further crediting to its
on-siteness and historical specificity while presenting the artist as an
observer of local practices. Therefore, Daniell devotes his efforts in
achieving absolute visual accuracy instead of inserting personal
interests.

However, while this eyewitness account justifies his narrative accuracy,
subsequent process of engraving and publication and its function as
souvenirs from Daniell's travels may change the appearance of this
print.[^7] The text of "Published & Sold, July 30, 1804, by Edwd Orme,
His Majesty's Printseller, 59 New Bond Street" at the bottom of the
image demonstrates this change. When subsequent processes were completed
in Britain, this aquatint may be susceptible to changes in its portrayal
of the Indian landscape under incentives to legitimize British
governance over the subcontinent. As travel illustrations, this aquatint
circulated back and was reprinted onto more commodities traded in the
British market, subjecting the Indian landscape to commercial
consumption. Thus, in doing so, the picturesque introduces the
discrepancy between Daniell's original construction and the audience
reception of this landscape as entities ready for British consumption.

Thus, Daniell's stylistic rendering of *A Hindoo Place of Worship*
reveals the tension in his depiction of an Indian landscape via
reframing the unfamiliar Indian landscape within a British aesthetic and
negotiating between its visual accuracy and audience reception.
Daniell's choices of proportion between the human figures and the
landscape redirect attention from the figures' sheer exoticism to their
harmonious integration in nature. The negotiation between the visual
accuracy of on-site production and its pictorial reception further
complicates Daniell's portrayal of the Indian landscape to the British
audience. Ultimately, the picturesque grapples with these complications
of the portrayal of the Indian landscape by a British artist during the
mercantile control of the British East India Company, revealing the
discrepancy between recording and representation.
