# Imagining the Mughal Court: European Reconstruction of the Weighing Ceremony in *Ceremony of Weighing the Great Mogul*

<small>Vicky Wang</small>

The importation of Indian goods, along with both written and visual
accounts by travelers, introduced readers to distant landscapes and
customs, stimulating their curiosity about these foreign cultures. One
such artwork is the engraving *Ceremony of Weighing the Great Mogul*,
published in John Hamilton Moore's *A New and Complete Collection of
Voyages and Travels* (1785), a compilation of travel accounts that
covers voyages to regions across multiple continents. 

Within the context of
Moore's book, the depiction of the weighing ceremony in the print can
easily be mistaken for a documentary representation of the ritual.
However, visual comparisons between the print and other artworks
depicting the same ceremony reveal that it distorts the historical
event. By transforming a formal court ritual into an exotic spectacle,
the engraving reflects European fascination with the "Orient" and
reinforces stereotypes of the Mughal Empire as despotic and
antiquated.

*Ceremony of Weighing the Great Mogul* depicts the weighing ceremony,
a significant ritual of the Mughal court. Traditionally performed on
the emperor's solar and lunar birthdays, the ceremony involved
weighing the emperor against valuable substances, such as gold,
spices, and food, with the equivalent weight in goods then distributed
to the public as charity. Some
accounts also record the ceremony being performed outside of birthdays
as a means of averting the evil influence of adverse astrological
events. Seventeenth-century
records of the ceremony emphasized the emperor's wealth and
generosity---charitable acts were regularly performed in Mughal
courts, and Indian depictions of the ceremony place it within an
opulent courtly setting, surrounded by courtiers and
officials.

In contrast to these historical descriptions, *Ceremony of Weighing
the Great Mogul* presents an impressionistic and largely inaccurate
illustration of the event. At the center of the scene, the Mughal
emperor, termed as the "Great Mogul," stands upright on one pan of a
monumental scale, while attendants place metal weights on the opposite
pan. A large crowd gathers around the scale---a few figures kneel
before the emperor in supplication, while guards and courtiers scatter
about, some conversing, some celebrating, and others merely witnessing
the scene.

In depictions of the ceremony made by Mughal court painters, such as
the Indian painting *Emperor Jahangir weighing his son Khurram in
gold*, as well as the early eighteenth-century European diorama of the
Mughal emperor Aurangzeb's birthday, *The Throne of Grand Mogul
Aurengzeb*, the court is portrayed with ceremonial order (see fig. 2).
In the former, courtiers stand in orderly rows observing the ritual,
while trays of jewels, gold, and other precious goods are arranged
neatly before the scale. In the latter, the figures are positioned
according to their rank, with the emperor seated on his throne at the
center of the scene, atop a staircase with a row of guards on each
end, while visiting princes kneel before him at the bottom. Both the
Indian painting and the European diorama demonstrate control and order
in their compositions rather than dramatic action. Neither of the
works presents the Mughal emperor as so accessible, nor the court as
so chaotic, as seen in *Ceremony of Weighing the Great Mogul*. The
engraving, rather than conveying the ritual's charitable and
ceremonial nature, presents a theatrical scene that aligns with the
narrative of the Mughal emperor forcefully demonstrating power.
Notably, the emperor's standing posture in the engraving is
particularly anomalous: in both comparative works, the central figure
is shown seated (in the diorama, though the emperor is not on the
scale, he is seated on his throne). These deviations suggest that the
depiction in *Ceremony of Weighing the Great Mogul* was likely not
drawn from firsthand observation, but is instead an imaginative
reconstruction designed to evoke awe and exoticism through a fanciful
rendering of the ritual.

The setting of the engraving further confirms that it is a product of
imagination rather than observation. In *Jahangir weighing his son
Khurram*, the setting is richly decorated with carpets featuring
intricate patterns, gold and jewel inlays, and Chinese
porcelain. Even in the
European diorama, *The Throne of Grand Mogul Aurengzeb*, the setting
is lavishly decorated with gold, silver, and thousands of jewels and
pearls. By contrast, *Ceremony
of Weighing the Great Mogul* depicts the ceremony as taking place in a
temporary tent-like space formed by draped curtains. Ornaments are
minimal, consisting of a bare tiled floor and plain fabric backdrops.
The weighing scales are unadorned, and the emperor is weighed against
metal weights rather than valuable goods. This contrast becomes even
more apparent when the engraving is compared with the written account
that accompanies it, which describes that "the palace is adorned with

The imaginative reconstruction of the weighing ceremony in *Ceremony
of Weighing the Great Mogul* was not an isolated depiction---it
participated in a broader shift in European attitudes toward India.
Over the course of the eighteenth century, European attitudes toward
the Mughal empire gradually shifted from fascination to increasing
criticism of its lavishness and perceived stagnation.
Seventeenth-century accounts of India often described the Mughal court
as an impressive display of wealth and power, emphasizing its splendor
and grandeur. By the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries,
however, European writers began to interpret the Mughal court more
critically: the weighing ceremony was portrayed as an excessive
ceremony through which the Mughal emperor sought to "obtain, or even
to force," gifts from his guests while offering only trivial rewards
in ret. Such critiques
reduced the complex political and charitable purpose of the ritual to
mere "showmanship" and "a sham," and extended to the broader
conclusion that the splendor of the Mughal court was
insubstantial. As these
narratives circulated widely in Europe, they contributed to a growing
stereotype of the Mughal Empire as "weak, lavish, and
despotic. Artistic
depictions such as *Ceremony of Weighing the Great Mogul* and
compilatory texts like *A New and Complete Collection of Voyages and
Travels* both reflect and reinforce this shifting perception,
implicitly characterizing the ceremony as ancient, unpleasing, and
uncivilized.

In conclusion, the engraving *Ceremony of Weighing the Great Mogul*
reveals more about European perceptions of India than about Mughal
court customs. The image's theatrical composition, simplified setting,
and inaccurate details shaped how contemporary readers understood---or
misunderstood---the historical ceremony. When compared with Mughal
court paintings and earlier European representations of the same
ritual, the engraving illustrates how visual culture participated in
constructing a European image of the "Orient." As Antje Flüchter
